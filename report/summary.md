# Knowledge Discovery, Summary Report

This is a short version of `report/ex1_first_layer.md`, `report/ex2_toscana.md`, `report/ex3_exploration.md`, `report/ex4_triadic.md`, `report/ex5_tca.md`, and `report/ex6_fca_application.md`. Exercises 1 to 5 each apply a different method to the same UCI Wine Quality (red) dataset, and this report pulls out what each one found and where they agree. Exercise 6 is a separate literature review of a real world FCA application, included here for completeness, not for direct comparison.

## Dataset

1,599 red wines, 11 chemistry attributes plus a quality score from 3 to 8, split throughout into Low (3-4), Medium (5-6) and High (7-8) tiers. A 200 wine sample was used for the FCA work in Exercises 2 and 4. Exercises 1 and 3 used the full 1,599 rows.

## Exercise 1: first layer knowledge discovery (clustering, Apriori, decision tree)

- **K-Means (k=3,4,5)** on standard chemistry values gives low scores for all three (0.189, 0.172, 0.190) that barely change with k. There is no clear number of natural clusters in the chemistry alone. Comparing clusters to the real quality tier shows every cluster, at every k, is mostly Medium quality (Medium is 82% of the data), and none is purely Low or High. **Clustering on chemistry alone does not recover quality.** The k=3 clusters still make chemical sense on their own: cluster 0 (722 wines, low fixed acidity and citric acid, high volatile acidity and pH, a lighter and less stable type), cluster 1 (502 wines, high fixed acidity, citric acid and sulphates, low volatile acidity and pH, a more robust type), cluster 2 (375 wines, high free and total SO2, more sugar, lower alcohol, a sweeter, more heavily treated type).
- **Apriori** (support 0.1, confidence 0.6) on 39 binned items gives 2,199 frequent itemsets and 7,800 rules. No rule concludes purely in `quality_tier_Low` or `quality_tier_High`, since both are small classes (3.9% and 13.6%) and a support of 0.1 needs at least 160 of 1,599 wines, a bar neither class clears with any single other item. 925 rules conclude in `quality_tier_Medium`, but their best lift is only about 1.17, so they mostly repeat the base rate. The one rule worth noting came from the other direction: `quality_tier_High -> citric_acid>=0.25` (confidence 78.8%, lift 1.51). High quality wines are 51% more likely than average to contain citric acid.
- **Decision tree** (depth 4): 87.4% train accuracy, 82.8% test accuracy. The top splits are alcohol (<=11.45) and volatile acidity (<=0.34), and both come up again at several points in the tree.

## Exercise 2: ToscanaJ conceptual information system

- 12 original attributes were scaled on the 200 wine sample, mostly using ordinal scales with increasing, include bounds settings (alcohol at 9/11/13%, sulphates at 0.4/0.6/0.8), single cut scales for citric acid, chlorides and density, and an interordinal scale for pH (3.1/3.4), chosen so both the acidic and basic sides of pH could be shown, which a one sided scale would have missed. This gives 33 binary attributes and a full concept lattice of **10,212 concepts**.
- The two required nested relationships (Quality with Alcohol, pH with Fixed Acidity) are presented as cross tables on the same 200 wine sample, giving the same information a nested diagram encodes. The Quality and Alcohol table shows Medium quality wines mostly at 9-11% alcohol (120 of 165), while Good quality wines lean more toward 11-13% (16 of 25), matching the same upward trend the Exercise 1 tree also found.
- 249 implications with a premise of size 1 or 2 were found, exact and complete for that size. 229 are real findings, 15 are trivial (they conclude in an attribute true for the whole sample), and 5 are scale artifacts. Five were discussed in detail: `citric_acid_absent -> volatile_acidity>=low` (support 100/200), `density=heavy -> fixed_acidity>=low` (93/200), `total_so2>=medium -> free_so2>=low` (35/200, expected since free SO2 is part of total SO2), `pH<=acidic -> fixed_acidity>=low and free_so2>=low` (21/200), and one with only 11/200 support showing that high volatile acidity still goes with at least acceptable quality if fixed acidity is also medium or higher and the wine is dry, a pattern only visible with two attributes together.
- pH and fixed acidity barely overlap at their extremes: 90% of the 20 most acidic pH wines have fixed acidity in the two highest bins, and 90% of the 52 most basic pH wines have it in the two lowest bins.

## Exercise 3: attribute exploration (wine domain, full dataset as the expert)

- 12 simple yes/no attributes were used (single cutoff versions of the Exercise 2 scales, for example `high_alcohol` means alcohol>=11%, `good_quality` means quality>=7), instead of the multi level scales, to avoid the result being full of trivial same attribute chains. The "expert" answering each proposed implication was the **full 1,599 row dataset itself**, so every decision was a real lookup and every counterexample was a real wine, not a personal guess.
- The exploration method checks all subsets in order of size instead of using a hand written version of the standard NextClosure algorithm. This gives the same correct result (a result only depends on smaller premises, and checking by size respects that order), and was chosen after an earlier hand written attempt, for the Exercise 2 base, had a hidden bug that was only caught by testing against a known example. With just 12 attributes (4,096 subsets), the simpler method runs almost instantly.
- The run took 282 steps and ended with a **123 implication base**, using 158 different wines as counterexamples, checked again afterward against the full dataset with 0 violations found.
- **Main finding**: no implication with a premise of size 1 or 2 survives against the full dataset, even though the 200 wine sample in Exercise 2 supported many such cases. The smallest implications that hold without exception need 3 conditions at once (29 of the 123, rising to 39 at size 4 and 40 at size 5, down to a single implication at size 11). This shows clearly that implications found on a sample are guesses about the full population, not settled facts, until checked at full scale.
- Among the 29 size 3 implications, `high_sulphates` is the most common result (8 of 29, for example `good_quality, high_fixed_acidity, high_volatile_acidity -> high_sulphates`), and `citric_acid_present` is second (5 of 29). Sulphates had not stood out this clearly in Exercises 1, 2 or 4, which had pointed more to alcohol and volatile acidity. No implication has `good_quality` with fewer than 2 other attributes, which fits with Exercise 1's clustering result that quality is not a simple function of one or two measurements.

## Exercise 4: triadic knowledge discovery

- The triadic context has 200 wines (G), 30 chemistry only attributes (M, the quality based attributes are left out since quality is now the condition), and the tiers Low, Medium, High as conditions (B). Each wine is linked only to its own tier, so the wines split cleanly into three separate groups. This single fact shapes every other result in this exercise.
- The three dyadic slices are K_Low (8 wines, 43 concepts), K_Medium (165 wines, 4,781 concepts), K_High (27 wines, 599 concepts). Concept count roughly follows wine count and variety.
- **Triconcepts reduce to the three separate dyadic lattices.** This was proven (a triconcept with a non empty extent cannot span two tiers, since no wine belongs to two tiers) and then checked directly: all 599 High tier concepts pass as valid triconcepts, and none stay valid once the condition set is widened to two tiers, matching the proof. The first version of this check had a mistake (it tested if a wine's tier was one of several tiers, instead of checking it matched all of them), which was caught and fixed.
- **Condition implications carry no real information here.** Since no wine belongs to two tiers, this type of implication is either true by default (when no wine satisfies the starting property in that tier) or false for every other tier (when at least one wine does). For example, `Low -> alcohol>=13` is true by default since no Low wine has that much alcohol, but `citric_acid_present`, which has wines in all three tiers, makes the implication false everywhere except trivially. This shows how the choice of incidence relation decides which implication types can give useful information.
- **Attribute implications do give tier specific results**: of 191 High tier implications, 163 do not hold under the same premise in Medium. For example, `fixed_acidity>=medium -> citric_acid_present` holds in High but not in Medium.
- **Object implications**: the Low tier has zero pairs of wines where one's properties are fully contained in another's, out of its 8 wines, while Medium has 318 such pairs and High has 5. A High tier example shows two wines that are identical except one also has `alcohol>=11`.
- Looking at which concepts stay the same versus which are tier specific: only 4 concepts appear in all three tiers, while 232 appear only in High. The High only example is marked mainly by `alcohol>=11`, the same attribute already flagged by the Exercise 1 tree and the Exercise 2 table.

## Exercise 5: Temporal Concept Analysis (optional)

- The dataset has no real time axis, so quality tier (Low, then Medium, then High) was used as a stand in for time, clearly marked as a simplification, not real time based data. A more direct problem, building on Exercise 4's proof that no wine belongs to more than one tier: no single wine can have a normal life track, since there is no object that exists across more than one stage. The fix used here is to track concepts instead of wines across the tier sequence, matching by their attribute set, the same kind of honest workaround used in Exercise 4 for its own unusual result.
- The three lattices from Exercise 4 were rebuilt on their own, and the sizes matched exactly (43, 4,781, 599 concepts for Low, Medium, High).
- Each concept's move into the next stage was labelled stable, refined, split, died or born. Both transitions gave **zero splits**, which was checked against two simple made up test cases (a real fork and a simple chain) before trusting it. Every concept that continues does so along one single path, it never forks.
- **Low to Medium** (42 real Low concepts): 23 stable, 13 refined, 6 died, and 3,666 born. Medium's lattice is so much richer than Low's that most Medium combinations have no match at all in Low.
- **Medium to High** (4,780 real Medium concepts): 364 stable, 2,681 refined, 1,735 died, and zero born. Every High combination already existed in some form in Medium, so High is defined by which Medium combinations survive, not by anything new.
- One example that dies going from Low to Medium combines `volatile_acidity>=high(0.7)`, a known fault marker, with other attributes, and this exact mix has no continuation into Medium at all. One example that gets refined gains exactly `alcohol>=11` on its way into Medium, matching alcohol's role in quality found elsewhere in this project.

## Exercise 6: report on a real world FCA application

- Not a hands on exercise on our own data, but a review of an existing, peer reviewed, real world FCA application, chosen to stay close in theme to our wine project without copying it.
- **Source**: Valente, Bauer, Venter, Watson and Nieuwoudt (2018), "Modelling the sensory space of varietal wines," *Scientific Reports* 8:4987. FCA was applied to about 7,000 real commercial Chenin blanc and Sauvignon blanc wines' free text tasting notes (John Platter Wine Guide, South Africa, 2008-2014), to build a sensory style map without running a new tasting panel.
- **Formal context**: objects are individual wines, attributes are standard aroma words taken from free text (266 and 250 words per variety after cleaning). These attributes were naturally yes or no, a word either shows up in a note or not, unlike our project, where every attribute needed a scaling decision based on continuous chemistry.
- **FCA's role**: a visual concept lattice (wines and descriptors as nodes), combined with three statistical methods it could not replace on its own: multidimensional scaling (which words tend to appear together), correspondence analysis (which words connect to which style), and CART (clear prediction rules, for example oak, rich, citrus, spice and vanilla pointing to wooded Chenin 73% of the time).
- **Checking the method**: it recovered the already known Sauvignon blanc sensory structure (capsicum, gooseberry, the herbal versus fruity split) as a sanity check, then gave a genuinely new result for Chenin blanc, with complexity as the main thing that separates styles, something not documented before for that variety.
- **A parallel with our own project**: both this paper and our Exercises 1 to 5 learned the same lesson on their own, that FCA alone is never the whole method. We paired it with checking which implications were scale artifacts, a decision tree, Apriori, and a full exploration over the whole dataset. This paper paired it with MDS, correspondence analysis and CART. In both cases, FCA's job was the same: giving a clear map of the attribute space, while the other methods supplied the numeric proof the map alone could not give.

## Where the methods agree

The most useful result of running five separate methods on the same data is what they agree on, since each one finds structure in a different way: a decision tree, implications from a sample, implications from the full dataset, triadic concept comparison, and concept level life tracks over a simulated timeline.

Alcohol's role in pushing quality up shows up in several of these methods. The decision tree puts it at the top split, the Quality and Alcohol table in Exercise 2 shows the same upward trend, the High only concept in Exercise 4 is marked by it, and in Exercise 5 it is exactly the attribute gained by the one example that gets refined moving from Low to Medium. Volatile acidity tells a related but different story: its effect on quality depends on other attributes rather than acting alone, which is why the decision tree needs more than one split on it, why Exercise 2's implications only show its effect once paired with another attribute, and why its presence in Exercise 5 marks a profile that dies out rather than one that continues into a better tier.

Citric acid and sulphates both come up as linked to quality, though through different methods. Citric acid's link to good chemistry appears in the Apriori rule from Exercise 1 (lift 1.51), in implication 1 from Exercise 2, and in 5 of the 29 smallest implications from Exercise 3. Sulphates only stood out clearly in Exercise 3, where they are the most common result among the same 29 smallest implications (8 of 29), a signal the other exercises had not picked up as strongly on their own.

Finally, several methods agree that quality is not decided by one or two attributes alone. The K-Means clustering in Exercise 1 only separates wines weakly, Exercise 3 finds no implication of size 1 or 2 that survives against the full dataset, Exercise 4's object implications need two or more conditions before they hold, and Exercise 5 shows that High tier concepts mostly survive or die off rather than appearing as something new. Five different methods landing on the same handful of chemical drivers, alcohol, volatile acidity, citric acid and sulphates, is a useful check on the result, since none of these methods were built to copy the others.

## Honest limitations

- Exercise 2's implications come from a 200 wine sample. Exercise 3 shows that several similarly small implications do not survive against the full 1,599 wine dataset, so Exercise 2's findings should be read as observations on a sample, not facts about the whole population.
- Exercise 4's condition implications carry no real information, given how the triadic incidence was defined. This comes from the modeling choice required by the assignment, it is not a mistake.
- Exercise 5's time axis is simulated, with quality tier standing in for time, and its life tracks are at the concept level, not the wine level, since no wine exists across more than one tier (the same fact proven in Exercise 4). This is a clearly stated substitute, not the standard definition of a TCA life track.
